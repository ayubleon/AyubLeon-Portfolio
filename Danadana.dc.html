Danadana

Overview

Danadana is the first Kenyan-made crash game, built locally rather than licensed and reskinned from elsewhere, and built around football, a sport its target audience actually loves.

Players watch a multiplier rise as an avatar keeps the ball in play, and cash out before it crashes or lose the stake. I was the sole designer on a small four-person team: one backend developer, one frontend developer, one animator, and me. Danadana is sold B2B — licensed to gambling operators who theme it to their own brand, rather than run directly by us to end players.

My role

I owned every screen and every component in the product, working directly with the animator and sound designer to make sure the visual and audio language actually served the mechanic. Because the product is white-labeled, I also owned the theming system that lets a single engine be re-skinned per operator without breaking the underlying UX.

Led research before any design work started: casino and online gambling psychology, competitor crash games, what features they'd tried and dropped and why, and informal user testing with a small group of actual crash game players.

Extended that research into sound and animation psychology, handing usable notes to the animator and sound designer rather than leaving it to guesswork.

Designed the small but critical component set: buttons, color, animation timing, and sound, since a game this simple has almost no room for a component to underperform and still convert.

The advantage

The advantage that made it work

A four-person team meant a short feedback loop. We ran impromptu meetings and used a shared Slack channel where anyone could throw in an idea. Some sessions were explicitly no-judgment idea sprints, which is where we solved problems other crash games hadn't, including a system to filter spam out of the live chat panel.

Challenges

Navigating challenges & quality control

Prioritizing functionality for licensing

The team faced a tight timeline to ship a working MVP required for official gambling permits and licenses. To meet the deadline without sacrificing UX integrity, we made a deliberate call to prioritize functionality first.

Balancing engagement with player protection

Because gambling carries real risk, we built a session-length safeguard directly into the engine: continuous play past 5 hours triggers a temporary lockout. Rather than cutting players off silently, we show an explicit message explaining why — an unexplained lockout in a gambling product reads as arbitrary or punitive rather than protective, and getting that message right mattered as much as the mechanic itself.

Safeguarding quality

I authored comprehensive design documentation and exact spacing/finishing specifications to ensure the dev team could execute the post-MVP polish without visual drift or guesswork.

Impact

Business & workflow impact

Research done up front meant design decisions were grounded in actual gambling psychology, not assumptions.

A tight, close-knit team let ideas move from whiteboard to build fast, with real problems (like chat spam) solved before launch instead of after.

The MVP cleared the permits and licenses required to operate, and is now being pitched to gambling operators for licensing.

Despite no longer working there day to day, I'm still consulted by the directors as they pursue their first operator signings and prepare for rollout, a sign the system I built is holding up under real sales conversations.
